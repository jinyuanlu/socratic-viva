# Domains: one method, many kinds of topic

The method is the same for every topic. A learner shows
understanding in three ways, in rising order of difficulty:

1. **Perform**: do the central thing, or state the central result.
2. **Justify**: say why it is so, or why it was done that way.
3. **Transfer**: handle a case not met before.

Tutoring practises all three. An exam ticket has one part for each
(Core, Foundations, Transfer). The charter's `Kind` decides what
the three look like in practice. Use the table, then the notes for
that kind. For `mixed` or anything not listed, derive the three
parts from the definitions above.

| Kind | Core (perform) | Foundations (justify) | Transfer |
|---|---|---|---|
| `concepts` | State and explain a central idea or result | Derive it, or explain why it must be true | A new case, a changed assumption, or a plausible but wrong claim to judge |
| `problem-solving` | Solve an unseen problem, reasoning aloud | Why the method is correct, what it costs, when it fails | The same problem with a constraint changed |
| `language` | A sustained speaking or writing task in the target language | Say the same thing under a change: other tense, person, register, or without a key word | An unprepared exchange with a complication |
| `interview` | Answer the question as in the real interview | Defend it under follow-up: specifics, reasons, alternatives, numbers | A changed requirement, a hypothetical, or the same case from another side |

## Concepts

Theory of any field: mathematics, sciences, engineering, history,
law, economics, a codebase, a standard, a book.

- Syllabus areas are ideas and results, not chapters.
- Prefer "why" and "what would change if" over "what is".

## Problem-solving

Skills shown by producing a solution: programming, mathematics
problems, estimation, case analysis, proofs, data analysis.

- Check the learner's actual work, not their description of it.
  Where tools are available, run the code or the query.
- Draw problems fresh. A well-known problem measures memory.
- A correct result with reasoning the learner cannot defend is
  not a pass.

## Language

Learning a human language.

- Run the session in the target language as far as the learner's
  level allows. Give explanations and records in the working
  language.
- Syllabus areas are abilities, for example "narrating past
  events", "polite requests", "reading news articles", together
  with the grammar and vocabulary they need. Avoid a syllabus that
  is only a list of grammar points.
- Do not ask for grammar terminology unless the learner's goal
  needs it. Many fluent speakers cannot name the rules they use.
  The Foundations part tests control of the system by asking for a
  controlled change, not for a description.
- In tutoring, do not correct every error. Correct what blocks
  meaning and what belongs to the current area; note the rest for
  later. In the exam, correct nothing.
- If the learner follows a published scale (CEFR, JLPT, HSK, TOPIK,
  ACTFL or another), write it in the charter's `Standard`. A
  verdict is then an estimate against that scale's public
  descriptions. It is not an official result of any test.
- Limits: a text session cannot assess pronunciation or listening.
  A verdict certifies only the abilities that were exercised, and
  must say which were not.

## Interview

Preparing for a job, admission, or oral examination interview. Ask
for the role, the level, and the interview formats expected, and
write them in the charter (`Standard` and `Sources`).

- Syllabus areas are the formats and their parts, for example
  "algorithmic coding", "system design", "behavioural questions",
  "explaining past projects", "questions to ask the interviewer".
- Technical formats follow *problem-solving* or *concepts*, with
  interview conduct: the learner asks clarifying questions, states
  assumptions, and reasons aloud. Weak communication of a correct
  answer is an edge, and the verdict names it.
- Behavioural formats: the Core part is the learner's own account
  of a real situation. The Transfer part is a hypothetical, or the
  same account seen from another person's position. In an exam,
  every account gets the same four fixed follow-ups, in these
  words and in this order, before any other probe: "What did you
  do yourself?", "Why that, and not something else?", "What else
  was possible?", "What was the result?". A point that appears
  only in answer to one of them is an edge in the account as
  first told.
- Work only with the learner's real experience. Help to select and
  structure true accounts. Never invent experience, results, or
  numbers for the learner, and do not coach them to.
- Customs differ between countries, industries, and employers. Do
  not assume one hiring culture. Ask the learner what applies.
- Limits: a verdict says how the answers held under probing. It
  does not predict the decision of any employer or institution.

## What cannot be examined here

A conversation can examine what can be said, written, or run. For
a physical or practical skill (an instrument, a sport, a craft,
laboratory or clinical practice) only the knowledge behind it can
be examined. Say so in the charter under `Out of scope`, and never
write a verdict that claims more.
